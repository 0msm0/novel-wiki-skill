# Episode 1: The Brass Thief

Port Hallow stank of salt and lamp oil, and Mira Osk loved it the way you love a dog that bites.

She worked the Fishmarket Stair at dusk, when the lantern-men climbed their ladders and every face in the crowd tipped upward to watch the lights come on along the Lantern Coast. Upturned faces meant unguarded pockets. Nineteen years old, short enough to vanish behind a fishwife's basket, and quick in her left hand. Her right palm still carried the shiny burn scar from the night her mother's stall caught fire, the night Ada Osk did not come out.

"Grey coat, top of the stair," Tobin Reed murmured at her shoulder. He was taller than her by a head and worse at hiding it. They had grown up under the same leaking roof in the Weir, and he still thought of himself as the one who kept her out of trouble. "Warden. Don't."

"Wardens have the best pockets," Mira said.

The Grey Wardens kept the harbour for the Harbour Council. They wore long grey coats with a silver hook stitched on the collar, and they hanged thieves from the Gull Gate on the first day of every month. This one was older, his beard cropped to iron stubble, and he carried something wrapped in oilcloth under his arm like a man carrying a baby he did not want.

She bumped him on the stair. Said sorry. Was gone.

Behind a net-mender's shed she unwrapped the cloth. Inside sat a compass of old brass, heavier than it looked, its glass cracked in a star. There was no N on the dial. Instead there were small engraved waves, and the needle did not point north. It swung, slow and deliberate, out toward the black water past the breakwater, and stayed there.

"That's not a compass," Tobin said. "That's a hanging."

"It's a fortune." But her mouth had gone dry. The needle pulled at her the way a held breath pulls.

A woman was standing at the end of the alley. Mira had not heard her come. She wore a hooded sea-cloak the colour of wet slate, and the hood hid everything but a pale chin and a thin silver ring through her lower lip.

"Put it back in the cloth," the hooded woman said. Her accent was from somewhere north, all soft edges. "It points at drowned things. If you hold it naked after dark, eventually it will point at you."

"Who are you?"

"Someone who was going to steal it before you did." The woman tilted her head toward the stair, where whistles had started up. "That was Captain Varr Dane you robbed. He will close the harbour by morning. If you want to live, be on the salt barge at the Weir landing before the second bell."

She tossed something. Mira caught it on reflex: a folded letter sealed in blue wax, with no name on it.

When she looked up the alley was empty.

They read the letter by the light of Tobin's stub of candle in the room under the roof. It said only: *The compass knows the way to Ilith. Do not let the Wardens take it south.* No signature. The handwriting slanted backward, as if the writer had been left-handed and in a hurry.

"Ilith is a story," Tobin said. "A city that sank because it was greedy. My gran used to tell it to scare me off the water."

"Your gran also said fish could hear lies."

Outside, the Gull Gate bell began to ring the closing of the harbour, hours early. Mira wrapped the compass in its oilcloth, and under her hand, through the cloth, she felt the needle turn.

It was pointing at the Weir landing.
